How we measure

Which tools we use, at which points, who collects the data — and what these measurements do not prove. Illustrated with real donor reports from 2023–2025.

What we measure and what we don't

We measure two different things, and they are often confused.

Volume — how many people, how many consultations, how many camp participants. This is the easiest thing to count, and it is what most reports show. Volume says nothing about whether anyone is better off.

Change in condition — whether a person's indicators changed after they received help. This is the whole point. We have measured it systematically since 2023, when we introduced the PHQ-9 at intake.

What we measure with

ToolWhat it showsWhen
PHQ-9, a standardised depression-symptom questionnaireseverity; a change of at least one level counts as improvementbefore the first session and after the course
Post-session feedback, online, named or anonymoussatisfaction and problems with the specialistafter every consultation
Mothers' survey before and after camp, plus specialist observationchanges in anxiety, emotional state and family communicationfirst and last day of camp
Daily camp feedback: QR form, anonymous questionnaires, a box, spokenwhat to change right nowevery day

We consider the last one the most honest signal of quality: people pay for what helps them.

Who collects the data

Feedback is read by a moderator, not by the specialist it concerns. The moderator acts on negative feedback directly: contacting the specialist and, if needed, assigning the client someone else. People complete the PHQ-9 themselves.

Donors receive aggregated figures only. No identifiable record is passed to anyone.

What our measurements do not prove

This is the most important section on the page. It is here because most reports leave it out.

There are no control groups. We compare a person with themselves before the work began, not with someone who received no help. So we cannot separate the effect of our work from the natural course of things, from time, or from changing circumstances.

The 90% covers one quarter and 201 people. It is the figure for the first three months of a year-long project. We will publish the annual figure when we have it.

There is little feedback compared with the number of people. In the IRC project 451 people received help and 69 left feedback — roughly one in seven. People who are satisfied, or very dissatisfied, are more likely to respond. 97% is the share among those who answered, not among everyone.

The PHQ-9 is not everywhere. In the Medair project the test was given at the start and the end, but the report does not state the result as a number. The mothers' survey at camp gave a qualitative picture without scores. Where there is no number, we do not make one up.

People who didn't make it through are not measured. People who applied but never reached a first consultation, or who dropped out, are missing from the data. If their condition differs systematically from those who stayed, every figure we have is biased. By how much, we don't know.

We don't know how people are doing six months later. Measuring over that horizon takes resources we do not yet have.

What we publish

  • Projects: goals, plans and actual results for every project, including where the plan was not met.
  • What we got wrong: mistakes, and what we did about them.
  • How the headline figures are counted, shown under the figures themselves in the Impact section rather than in a separate document.

We do not publish anonymised datasets. That would need an anonymisation policy with a minimum cell size, which the foundation does not yet have. Publishing data about a vulnerable group without one is a risk, not a contribution to knowledge.
