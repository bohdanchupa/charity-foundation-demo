Sleeplessness after a traumatic event

Sleep breaks first and recovers last. What you can do about it yourself, what is worth avoiding, and when it stops being a question of routine.

If you have not slept properly since something happened · 6 min read

If you have not slept for several days running, or the sleeplessness comes with thoughts of harming yourself, this is not about routine. Call.

Why sleep breaks first

To fall asleep the body needs permission: it is safe now, control can be switched off. After a traumatic event that permission is not granted. The brain keeps watch — and the harder you try to force sleep, the more clearly you signal that something is wrong.

Hence the first and most important thing: trying to fall asleep is the worst available method of falling asleep.

What works

Get up at the same time. Every day. Even after a bad night. The waking hour is the only point you can control deliberately; the falling-asleep hour follows it within a few days. Sleeping until noon after a bad night wrecks the next one and closes the loop.

The bed is for sleep only. Not for the phone, not for the news, not for lying there thinking. If you have not fallen asleep in about twenty minutes, get up, go to another room, do something dull in dim light, come back when you feel drowsy. It is unpleasant for the first few nights and it works.

A dedicated worry hour in the daytime. Twenty minutes at a set time, with pen and paper: what is worrying you, what can be done about it tomorrow, what cannot. Thoughts that arrive at three in the morning arrive because there was no room for them during the day.

The body needs to be tired. Physical effort during the day, not in the evening.

Dark and cool. Obvious, but a cool dark room does more than any supplement.

What to avoid

Alcohol. Falling asleep really is easier. But it suppresses the phase in which the brain processes what happened, and the second half of the night falls apart into wakings. You feel worse in the morning than without it, and the connection is invisible, because "I fell asleep fine".

Sleeping pills without a doctor. Some over-the-counter preparations produce dependence within two weeks and make the sleeplessness worse once stopped.

Screens in bed. Not only because of the light — because a news feed at night returns the body to watch-keeping more precisely than anything else.

Counting the hours. Turn the clock to face the wall. "Four hours left" is itself a stimulant.

A note on dreams

Dreams about the event are not a sign that things are getting worse. They are the mind's attempt to process what it could not process in the daytime. They usually become less frequent on their own.

If the same nightmare repeats every night for months and you wake from it in panic, tell a specialist. There are specific methods for post-traumatic nightmares that work, and none of them consists of "thinking about it less".

When it is no longer about routine

If a month or six weeks after the event:

  • you sleep less than five hours on most nights;
  • you cannot function in the daytime through exhaustion;
  • you only fall asleep with alcohol or pills;
  • the nightmares repeat nightly,

— self-management has been exhausted. That is not a failure of discipline; it is a different order of problem.

What the foundation can offer

Work on sleep is part of an ordinary course. We do not treat insomnia in isolation: where it rests on trauma it resolves alongside the work on that, not separately from it. If medication is needed, that is not us, and we will say who it is.

These articles do not replace a consultation and do not diagnose. They describe typical reactions and what helps most people — your case may differ, and that is an ordinary reason to get in touch.
