For the family of someone who has come back from service

Coming home rarely looks the way it was imagined. What happens in the first months, what helps, and why you need support too, even when it seems you do not.

If your husband, wife, son, daughter or parent has come back from service · 8 min read

If they are talking about not wanting to live, there is a weapon in the house and they are in crisis — call now, do not wait for morning.

What to be ready for

The usual expectation is: he is back, so the worst is over. The reality is usually different: the hardest part is not the first days but the second and third month, when the reunion is over and life has not come back.

That does not mean something has gone wrong. It is the ordinary course.

What you may see

  • Distance. Physically present, absent in fact. It is not indifference to you and not cooling off.
  • Irritability and sharpness. The threshold is lowered; it fires on small things.
  • Alertness. A seat with the back to the wall, checking exits, reacting to sounds, broken sleep.
  • Loss of meaning. Everyday life feels small and pointless after somewhere the stakes were different.
  • Survivor's guilt. "Why am I here and they are not." Often never said out loud.
  • The pull to go back. Not because home is bad, but because there it was clear who you were and what you were doing.
  • Alcohol. The most common self-medication and the fastest way to complicate everything else.

What helps

Do not force a "return to normal life". There should be fewer plans, guests, celebrations and conversations about the future than you want. Overload looks like withdrawal.

Do not ask for the story. "Tell me what it was like" puts the person in front of a choice: lie, shut down, or hurt you with the truth. Let them know you are ready to listen whenever they want — and then do not come back to it.

Agree a signal. One sentence either of you can say to leave a conversation without explaining. This reduces outbursts more than any discussion of feelings.

Keep daily life predictable. The same dinner time, the same arrangements. Predictability works like treatment even though it does not look like it.

Give a role. Specific, manageable, genuinely needed. Feeling useless at home is one of the strongest factors in getting worse.

Do not issue ultimatums about therapy. "Either you see a psychologist or I leave" almost always produces refusal. What works is leaving the contact details in sight and saying it is your request, not a diagnosis.

About you

You are in this too. And you almost certainly do not count yourself among those who need help, because "I wasn't there".

Signs worth noticing in yourself: constant alertness and reading the other person's mood · sleep that broke along with theirs · giving up your own plans and friends · guilt about your own tiredness · the thought "I have to hold out, it is harder for him".

That is not weakness or selfishness. A person living beside the consequences of trauma every day runs down — and exhausted, you help less well.

About children

A child notices everything and explains it to themselves in the worst way: dad is angry because I am bad. Say it directly: dad has changed, it is not because of you, it is hard for him now, and he is working on it.

Do not make a child the intermediary — "go and tell dad", "be quiet because of dad". That puts the adult's state on their shoulders.

When to act without waiting

  • talk of not wanting to live, of saying goodbye, giving belongings away;
  • there is a weapon in the house and the person is in crisis;
  • alcohol or medication every day;
  • physical aggression — any, including once;
  • the person has not left the room for weeks.

In these cases you should reach out, even if they refuse. Asking for help on someone else's behalf is allowed, and it is not a betrayal.

What the foundation offers

The veterans stream runs peer support groups led by a pair: a veteran facilitator and a psychologist. That is not a formality — on our own figures, 74 % of participants reach the sixth meeting in groups with a veteran facilitator against 41 % where a specialist leads alone. The sample is small and the comparison uncontrolled, but the gap was enough to change the model.

There are separate groups for spouses and adult children. It is the least-reached group precisely because the people in it do not count themselves among those who need help.

These articles do not replace a consultation and do not diagnose. They describe typical reactions and what helps most people — your case may differ, and that is an ordinary reason to get in touch.
