Anger and irritability: why it is a symptom, not a character trait

Snapping at the people closest to you after a traumatic event is one of the most common sources of shame and one of the least discussed. What is actually happening, and what works.

If you snap at people you love and then blame yourself for it · 6 min read

If you are afraid you might harm someone close to you, that is a reason to ask for help today, not a reason for shame.

Where to start

After a traumatic event the threshold at which the fight-or-flight response fires drops. Not metaphorically — physiologically. What used to be trivial now triggers a full threat response.

So a person who never raised their voice starts shouting over spilt tea. It is not about the tea, and it is not that they have "gone bad".

Why it almost always lands on your own people

Because at work, in the street, with strangers, you hold it together. Holding costs resource, and by evening the resource is gone. At home you do not have to keep a face on, so that is where it breaks through.

This does not mean the people at home are to blame. It means you spend all your control outward and arrive home without any.

What happens forty seconds before

An outburst does not come from nowhere. It is preceded by a sequence you can learn to notice:

  1. Body. Jaw and shoulders tighten, fists clench, heat in the face, breathing shortens.
  2. Attention. It narrows. You stop hearing the words and hear the tone.
  3. Thought. Something like "he is doing this deliberately", "I am not respected", "how much longer".
  4. Action.

There is a gap between one and four. At first it feels instantaneous. It widens with practice.

What works

Leave. Physically. Not "calm down" — leave the room for two minutes. Not an escape from the conversation, a pause inside it. Tell the people you live with in advance that you will do this, or it reads as a performance.

Name it out loud. "I am about to snap, it is not about you, I need a minute." One sentence, agreed in advance. It does two things: it slows you down, and it stops the other person guessing what they did wrong.

Spend the charge in advance. Physical effort does not "relieve stress" in the abstract — it spends the same resource that would otherwise go into the outburst. Twenty minutes of brisk walking in the evening changes the evening.

Sleep. One sleepless night lowers the threshold more than any particular provocation.

Alcohol removes the brakes first. Not the anger — the capacity to hold it.

What to say to children

A child who is shouted at without a comprehensible reason explains it, by default, with themselves: I am bad, it is my fault. That lasts.

So after an outburst three things are needed, and they are not an apology for the sake of an apology:

  • name it — "I shouted";
  • lift the blame — "it is not because of you, it is because of what is going on with me";
  • say what you are doing — "I am working on it so it happens less."

The third matters. A child needs to know not only that it is not their fault but that the adult has hold of the situation.

When your own effort is not enough

  • outbursts are getting more frequent rather than less;
  • you have already done harm — physical, or the kind words cannot take back;
  • people close to you have started to be afraid of you;
  • you are afraid of yourself;
  • alcohol has become the way not to snap.

Any one of these is a reason to talk to a specialist, and none of them makes you a bad person. This is one of the most tractable symptoms: it responds to work noticeably faster than the others.

What the foundation does with it

It is part of ordinary trauma work — there is no separate "anger therapy". For many people irritability is the first thing to ease, often before sleep comes back.

These articles do not replace a consultation and do not diagnose. They describe typical reactions and what helps most people — your case may differ, and that is an ordinary reason to get in touch.
