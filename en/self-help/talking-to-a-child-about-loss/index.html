Talking to a child about loss

What to say when someone close has died, what never to say, and why silence is not protection but the worst of the available options.

If there has been a death in your family and you do not know how to talk to a child about it · 8 min read

If a child talks about not wanting to live, that is not "they do not understand what they are saying". Call today.

The most common mistake

Not telling them. Or saying "he has gone away", "he is asleep", "we will talk about it later".

The intention is clear: to protect. The result is the opposite. A child always knows something has happened — from the voices, from who came, from how they are being looked at. If they are given no explanation they will build their own. A child's explanation is almost always worse than the truth and almost always contains the child: because of me, I did something wrong, I thought something bad.

The truth, in simple words, is lighter than that.

How to say it

In plain words. "Dad has died. He is not coming back." Not "gone", not "asleep", not "we lost him". Euphemisms work literally in a small child: "asleep" makes sleep frightening, "gone away" means he might return, and they will wait.

Briefly. One or two sentences, then a pause. A child takes as much as they can take now. The rest comes over the following days, and they will ask.

From someone they trust, somewhere safe. Not in a corridor, not on the phone, not from a stranger.

Let them ask. The questions will be strange: will he be cold, what will he eat, can we phone him. That is the ordinary work of a child's mind, not callousness.

Say who is here now. The biggest fear after losing one adult is losing the other. Say it directly: "I am staying with you." If it is true.

What never to say

  • "Don't cry", "be strong", "you're the man of the house now." The last takes away childhood and puts on a weight the child cannot lift. They hear it as an instruction to stop having feelings.
  • "He is looking after you from above", "God takes the best" — unless that is the ordinary language of your family. Otherwise it creates confusion, and sometimes anger at God that the child has nowhere to put.
  • "Everything will be fine." It will not be as it was. The child can see that, and the sentence only shows that this cannot be discussed with adults.
  • Nothing at all. Silence is worse than clumsy words.

How it looks at different ages

Under 5. Does not grasp irreversibility. Will ask about the same person many times. Reacts mostly through the body: sleep, food, toileting, clinging. Regression — returning to long-abandoned habits — is expected.

6–11. Grasps irreversibility and looks for a cause. This is where guilt most often appears: "if I had behaved". May look calm and go on playing — that is not indifference, it is rationing.

12–17. May close down entirely, dismiss it ("I don't care"), turn to anger, or take over the care of younger siblings. Looks the most composed from outside and is often the worst off inside. Needs availability rather than a conversation on demand — someone to come to when they choose.

What helps afterwards

  • Ritual. Taking part in the farewell if they want to, with an explanation beforehand of what will happen. Do not force it; do not forbid it either.
  • Keeping the ordinary. School, clubs, the timetable. Not to distract them, but so the whole world does not fall apart.
  • Permission to be happy. Children grieve in bursts: half an hour of crying, then play. That is normal, and laughter is not a betrayal.
  • Memory. Photographs, belongings, talking about the person in the present tense of recollection. Avoiding the name makes the loss larger, not smaller.

When a specialist is needed

  • after a few months nothing has shifted;
  • the child has stopped playing, talking to people, going to school;
  • there are persistent fears, bedwetting, aggression, self-harm;
  • they say they want to be with the person who died.

The last is a reason to call the same day.

What the foundation does

We work with children from the age of 7 — individually at the centre and at the rehabilitation rotations, which a child attends together with an adult. That is not incidental: the child returns to the same family, and if the adult's state has not changed, the effect of the rotation fades within weeks. So adults come as participants, not as chaperones.

These articles do not replace a consultation and do not diagnose. They describe typical reactions and what helps most people — your case may differ, and that is an ordinary reason to get in touch.
